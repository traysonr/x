PROJECT LOG / STARTED 2026.10

RTL-SDR radio automation

A Python based radio survey and packet analysis workbench for an RTL-SDR: scanning VHF/UHF channels, triaging captures, and figuring out what unknown signals are actually saying.

  • Python
  • RTL-SDR
  • Signal processing
  • RF

This started as a weekend idea: let a cheap USB radio sweep channels, save anything interesting, and help me sort the hits afterward. It has grown into a small desktop workbench for scanning, replaying captures, and poking at signals that don’t come with a decoder.

The receiver is an RTL-SDR. Python handles the scan and review workflow; rtl_433 helps identify supported devices, and small analysis scripts fill in the gaps when it doesn’t. A hit is a lead, not an identification: scan dwell time, antenna, local interference, and the receiver setup all affect what makes it into the log.

BUILD NOTES

Project log

CHRONOLOGICAL
04 Oct 2026

First sweep: make the radio do the boring part

The first version was a Python loop around the RTL-SDR: tune a channel, take a short look, and save a capture when the signal crossed a threshold. The goal was simple—cover the local VHF/UHF channels without sitting there retuning by hand.

The first broad grid had 5,601 channels. At a quarter second per probe, one pass could take roughly 23 minutes before tuner settling or file work. That was too slow for casual experiments, so I added focused profiles for the bands and frequencies I actually wanted to watch. A plotted sweep also made one strong, repeatable feature around 445.0375 MHz stand out. That was a useful lead, but a peak alone doesn’t tell me what modulation or user is behind it.

I also ran into a driver/API mismatch while setting frequency correction. Fixing the startup path got the receiver running reliably; after that, the bigger question was what to save when a hit appeared.

10 Oct 2026

Keep the I/Q; audio can wait

Saving only demodulated audio was a dead end for digital signals. I switched the hit recorder to keep raw complex samples alongside the NFM WAV, so I could come back later and try another filter or decoder without needing the radio to hear the transmission again.

That exposed a couple of practical wrinkles. The scan notices a signal before it starts the five-second capture, so a short transmission can be mostly gone by then. And for NFM, filtering the channel before FM demodulation made a much cleaner result than trying to demodulate the whole 1.024 MHz capture first. A nearby DMR repeater also explained why a strong hit could sound like hash on the analog audio path: strong RF isn’t the same thing as analog voice.

Keeping every full-rate capture quickly became a storage problem—one overnight run produced about 6.35 GB. That pushed storage and review into the design instead of treating captures as disposable debug files. I started narrowing the scan profile and keeping the raw recording where it could answer a real question.

11 Oct 2026

Known weather packets, then an unknown one

At 433.94 MHz, rtl_433 gave me a nice sanity check: it recognized repeating Oregon weather-sensor packets and produced structured readings. That confirmed the capture-and-decode path was working for at least one known format.

Then a targeted capture around 433.9125 MHz produced a pulse train that the built-in decoders didn’t recognize. I generated a local report with the pulse timing and signal plots, and started treating it as an unknown packet format instead of calling it a device. My first bit slicing was wrong; what looked like a short frame was a parsing artifact. Keeping the capture made it possible to revisit the same RF data as the parser improved.

The digital workflow now records raw I/Q and keeps the decoder’s report with the run. A pulse viewer is a useful clue, but it is not a protocol identification by itself.

12 Oct 2026

A checksum turned a pulse pattern into real frames

I went back to the original, unfiltered 60-second capture centered at 433.900 MHz. Two signals sat near 433.8885 and 433.9114 MHz. The first promising decode was mis-sliced; I had to stop trusting the bit string and look for structure that repeated.

The revised parser found a D3 91 sync word, read the following length field, and checked a CRC-16 before accepting a frame. It recovered 396 CRC-valid frames and rejected 67 apparent syncs that failed the check. The frames fall into two recurring groups: 84 short frames with payload 00, and 312 frames with payload 11 22 33. Both include the same sync and valid length/check fields.

That’s strong evidence for the framing, not an identity card for the transmitter. The header fields are still unknown, and 11 22 33 looks like a test pattern—but that last part is only a guess. Next useful experiment: capture while deliberately changing or powering the suspected device, then see which bytes move.

12 Oct 2026

Turn one-off scripts into a review console

Once the overnight scan started producing hundreds of candidates, I needed a place to review them without losing the trail. I built a small PySide desktop app with separate Basic Scan, Broad/Fast Scan, and Digital Scan modes, dated run folders, profiles, capture playback, and a shared encounter log.

The first classifier pass was deliberately a screen, not a verdict. Replaying the 2026-10-11 run with version 2.0 left 533 noise, 198 review, and 1 voice candidate. I kept the review bucket wide because weak audio and unknown modulation are easy to mislabel. A person can mark voice/no voice after listening; those labels are saved for future analysis, but they do not train the current classifier.

That distinction matters: the app can help sort a pile of captures, but only listening or a decoder can confirm what a particular hit was. The later storage cleanup also made clear that raw I/Q is valuable and expensive. Capture retention needs to be a conscious choice, not an accidental side effect of an overnight run.

17 Oct 2026

Faster sweeps, better questions for each hit

The serial scan was doing a lot of retuning just to ask whether anything was there. Broad/Fast now groups the 2,652 configured channel centers into 97 wide windows, checks each for about 0.128 seconds, and extracts up to the three strongest channels when a window crosses threshold. On paper that’s about 12.4 seconds of probe time for a pass, before USB/FFT work, tuner settling, and the five-second triggered dwells.

It makes the survey much quicker, with a tradeoff: a brief burst can fall between probes, and only the strongest few channels in a busy window make it to full capture. This is a way to find leads, not continuous coverage.

I also pulled review into the scan screens: sort and filter candidates, play original or enhanced audio, mark a human voice label, add a frequency note, or send a candidate to Digital Scan. Audio enhancement only affects in-memory playback; it leaves the original WAV alone. A recent seven-pass review had 19 candidates. Several repeated narrow peaks near 444.9 MHz looked like receiver spill/spurs, so I grouped them for review rather than calling them transmissions.

The satisfying part is that the tool is becoming more honest about uncertainty. A candidate table is a queue for the next experiment, not a list of solved signals.
